What I Built

Every year Kaggle runs a March Madness prediction contest where you submit win probabilities for every possible NCAA tournament matchup. You're scored on how well-calibrated your probabilities are (Brier score / MSE) — not just whether you picked the right winner, but whether your 70% picks actually win about 70% of the time.

I built a system that processes 16 seasons of NCAA game data (2010–2025, both men's and women's), engineers features from raw box scores, trains three different model types, and blends them into a single set of predictions. I then submitted 11 different strategies to Kaggle to test what actually works.

I also built an interactive bracket predictor (below) so anyone can explore the model's picks, compare matchups, or override them with their own.

Stack: Python, XGBoost, LightGBM, Scikit-learn, Pandas, FastAPI, Streamlit

How It Works

Feature Engineering

Each matchup is represented as the difference between two teams across 7 stats. The raw game data goes through a perspective-flipping step — every game generates two rows (one from each team's point of view) — which makes aggregation cleaner.

FeatureWhat It Captures
SeedSelection committee's ranking (lower = better)
Point DifferentialAverage scoring margin over the regular season
Offensive EfficiencyPoints per possession × 100
Win PercentageRegular season win rate
Elo RatingCustom rating with margin-of-victory adjustments and season carry-over
FG%Field goal shooting accuracy
FT%Free throw shooting accuracy

I also explored GLM team quality scores, late-season momentum, strength of schedule, and coach tournament history — they didn't improve holdout performance enough to justify the added complexity.

Models

I trained three model types and blended them. Each runs separately for men's and women's tournaments since they have different dynamics:

  • Logistic Regression (60% weight) — stable, well-calibrated out of the box. Tuned regularization: C=0.25 for men, C=0.15 for women.
  • XGBoost (20% weight) — picks up non-linear feature interactions. Uses a custom Cauchy loss function that's robust to blowout-margin outliers.
  • LightGBM (20% weight) — adds ensemble diversity. Tuned via randomized search.

The final probabilities are clipped to [0.025, 0.975] so no single wrong pick destroys the Brier score.

Validation

Training on 2010–2024, holdout on 2025. The best ensemble hit MSE 0.1571 on holdout — solid for tournament prediction where upsets are inherently unpredictable.

What I Learned

  • Simple models go a long way. Logistic regression with good features was hard to beat. The gradient-boosted models helped at the margins, but the biggest gains came from better feature engineering, not model complexity.
  • Calibration matters more than accuracy. The competition scores probability quality, not just correct picks. A model that says "65%" and is right 65% of the time beats a model that says "90%" and is right 75% of the time.
  • Men's and women's tournaments are different. Combined training consistently underperformed separate pipelines with different regularization strengths. The dynamics are genuinely different.
  • More submissions = more learning. Submitting 11 variants (from a 4-feature baseline to a 3-model ensemble) taught me more about what matters than any single model could have.
